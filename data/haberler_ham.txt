[1] The Hacker News - OpenRouter Drops 3 Providers After Researchers Catch Months of Prompt Theft at AI Hosting Firms
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Tarih: Sun, 22 Nov 2026 10:36:55 +0530
Link: https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/openrouter-drops-3-providers-after.html

[TAM METİN - 1430 kelime]
Attackers stole data from inside three AI inference providers for months, and none of the three knew until researchers told them, the industrial cybersecurity company Dragos said this week.

Some of the stolen requests were flagged as Zero Data Retention, the setting that is supposed to mean a prompt is never stored.

OpenRouter, a gateway that routes customers' AI requests to a pool of third-party providers, said today that it has removed three providers from its platform, removed 125 endpoints from eligibility for Zero Data Retention, and notified all potentially affected customers. The findings showed no breach of OpenRouter itself, the company said.

The earliest of the three breaches began in mid-July. The planted prompts were bait, but they were processed alongside real customer requests, and those were almost certainly copied too, the firm said.

The finding comes from canary token research that seeded about two million prompts with unique web links and credentials between July and November, then watched for anyone using them. A planted secret can only be learned in one place, so any use of it means someone read the prompt that carried it.

The largest of the three breaches involved a couple hundred AWS keys that were planted, each sent in exactly one request to one provider during a few short windows in late October. Every request sent during those windows was stolen, and some came from requests the provider had rejected before a model ever ran, indicating the attacker was copying traffic at the first point of arrival rather than deeper in the inference stack.

The thief tested keys within hours in some cases, listed S3 buckets, searched Secrets Manager and Parameter Store, and tried to create Bedrock API keys and add IAM users to keep access, driving tens of thousands of AWS API calls.

One attacker appears to have compromised two of the providers at once, using secrets from both from a single rented server.

On November 1, the same attacker brought in ARTEX, an AI-driven penetration testing framework, and used it only to confirm that keys it already held were still live. Two of its user agents, artex-agent and artex-judge-r121, appear in no public indicator list the firm could find and are worth adding to detections.

"A privacy flag doesn't help when the attacker is already inside," wrote HD Moore, the firm's chief research officer.

The second finding needed no break-in. Sellers on an AI inference marketplace listed models at the lowest prices, enabled request logging, and collected what flowed through, the research found.

The people doing it looked like individuals on home internet connections. One, working from a Mac on home broadband in France, took passwords from two prompts and typed root SSH login attempts by hand, then used the same machine to test several planted AWS keys.

One of those keys had traveled through a different router that quietly forwarded some of its traffic to the marketplace, a hop that no customer was told about. That router has since told the firm it stopped, and route tracing across about 2,000 paths found several hundred unidentified systems, plus one seller that quietly adds about 1,000 hidden tokens to every prompt.

A Chinese AI lab, which the research does not name, fetched the secret links from more than half of the seeded prompts sent to its models between July and October, including about two-thirds of the requests marked no data collection or Zero Data Retention. Prompts sent to U.S. hosting providers that resell the lab's models triggered the same scraper, thereby flowing back to the lab.

A git client on another cloud later tried to clone private repositories referenced in the prompts, but only for that lab's models, and one large U.S. provider kept crawling a secret link more than two months after a single request to its search endpoint.

Twenty-four days after one prompt was sent, its secret link was posted into a chat on Feishu, the Chinese workplace app known as Lark elsewhere. The link was opened within two minutes from three desktops and three phones in Beijing and Shanghai.

The firm had disclosed only the first 8 characters of the provider's link, so whoever posted it was reading a cached copy of the full prompt.

In practice, Moore wrote, the flagged prompts were kept, passed to other companies, and read by human beings.

OpenRouter said in an incident announcement that the 125 endpoints removed from Zero Data Retention eligibility cover providers flagged by the researchers plus others cut as a precaution. Any endpoint removed during the review will require assurances and validation before its status is returned.

The gateway is adapting the researchers' testing approach so that the checks can run on an ongoing basis and is verifying network paths to identify where requests and responses may be forwarded to third parties.

The researchers are now working with about a dozen providers, and planted keys and secret links were still being accessed months after the first thefts, as of this week.

Railway, a platform company, said in October that an intrusion it suffered was likely part of a series of attacks built to target platform companies with the end goal of compromising AI inference providers.

Not all affected providers have posted publicly, the gateway said, and it has notified affected users either way. Three have posted.

What the Providers Have Disclosed

An attacker took control of an inference server at Phala and could view request data while it was being processed, the provider said in a preliminary incident report last week. The incident was contained on November 8, and the model service has been restored with added protections.

Requests to the DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 model that reached Phala through the gateway between October 21 at 00:00 UTC and November 8 at 05:35 UTC should be treated as exposed, the report says, and every key, token, password, or other secret within them should be rotated.

A third-party researcher had been placing unique canary credentials in requests since July 1, and only the ones sent on October 21 and 22 were later misused. Those tests sampled traffic rather than monitoring every request, so the provider maintains the broader precautionary window.

At Together AI, an unauthorized party downloaded stored samples of customer prompts after an AI coding agent committed a credential to the company's public GitHub repository, the company said yesterday in a security notice. The samples had been collected for routine software testing and were generated by requests to the MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2, and Kimi K2.6 models in June and July.

The party searched the samples for credentials to other services and tested them, and five of the tested credentials were canaries from the researchers' experiment. The company said it has no reason to believe the data went further, understands it has been deleted, and has made zero data retention the default for new accounts.

Novita, another provider on the gateway, confirmed this week in a privacy notice that a routing configuration issue caused some MiniMax requests to be sent on to MiniMax's official API, beyond what was intended for those customers.

Dragos has not named the three breached providers, OpenRouter has not named the three it removed, and neither has said publicly whether they are the same three.

The provider behind the mid-July breach first blamed an internal misconfiguration, then a partner's data processing, before its own investigation found that attackers had compromised its internal credentials, the researchers said. It had still not disclosed the incident publicly as of this week.

The researchers' advice starts from one limit: a privacy flag binds only the first hop, Moore wrote, and AI agents will send whatever they can read, credentials included. Where a prompt goes is a choice, he wrote, and a customer who does not choose hands the choice to the lowest price.

Self-hosting is the only option in which no third party reads a prompt, and going directly to a first-tier provider leaves one company, one contract, and one privacy policy to check.

Routers need more care: pick the upstream providers yourself instead of accepting the default pool, verify that privacy settings actually change where requests go, and stay away from reseller marketplaces, some of whose capacity runs on stolen keys.

A fake credential seeded into the context that agents and pipelines already send serves as a tripwire if any use of it is treated as an incident, and a free Thinkst Canarytoken is enough to get started. Settings that scrub known credential formats from prompts help too, though they miss sensitive data that is not shaped like a key.

[2] The Hacker News - Researchers Demonstrate Ghostscript Sandbox-Escape RCE via Crafted File Uploads in Popular Apps
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Tarih: Sun, 22 Nov 2026 10:33:41 +0530
Link: https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/researchers-demonstrate-ghostscript.html

[TAM METİN - 764 kelime]
A now-patched flaw in Ghostscript allows a crafted file to escape the program's security sandbox and run commands on the server that processes it.

Ghostscript is the interpreter that many Linux servers use to convert uploaded PDFs, PostScript files, and images into other formats, often without the application calling it directly.

Security firm pwn.ai showed this week that ordinary upload, preview, and print features in about ten common applications could be tricked into feeding it such a file. The flaw, tracked as CVE-2026-125396, was fixed on September 8, and there have been no reports of it being used in attacks.

Getting a crafted file to Ghostscript is easy, because an application does not have to call it directly. A common image or document library such as ImageMagick, Pillow, libvips, or a Qt plugin will hand PostScript or EPS content to Ghostscript to render, even when the file is named .png, .jpg, or .pdf. A routine feature like making a thumbnail or cropping a picture can start the interpreter, and the exact path depends on the libraries the application uses.

The underlying bug is a use-after-free in the part of Ghostscript that loads fonts. A crafted document uses it to switch off the checks in -dSAFER, the sandbox that is supposed to stop the document from running commands or reaching files.

The security firm demonstrated the result by feeding such a document through about ten applications and watching a command run on the server, each time as the low-privilege account that does the rendering.

Several of those paths needed no login: an anonymous print job to a shared Debian 13 queue, an emailed attachment that Roundcube webmail processed the moment the recipient opened the message, and public upload forms in the WordPress plugins Forminator and Essential Addons for Elementor.

WordPress Core itself needed more: an account with the ability to upload files, which Authors and higher roles have by default. Although the CVE is classified as a local flaw, the firm's tests reached Ghostscript from the network via print jobs, email, and web uploads. The tested version and the access needed for each application are set out in its impact index.

Artifex, which develops Ghostscript, fixed the flaw in version 10.08.0 on September 8, and is moving the vulnerable internal step into safer code. Ken Sharp, the developer who handled the fix, called that step "an ancient piece of code well known to be a vulnerability, but difficult to eradicate" in the bug report. The CVE was published last week and is scored 8.5 out of 10 in its security advisory.

WordPress had already closed its own upload path in version 7.0.4 on August 12, under a separate identifier, CVE-2026-65640, as noted in its release notes. That fix and the Ghostscript fix cover different points in the same chain, so updating one does not remove the need for the other.

Updating the Renderer

The fix helps only where it reaches the renderer the application actually uses. Updating the host operating system does not update a separate copy of Ghostscript bundled inside a container image or an application, so a document worker can keep running the old code after the host package is replaced.

Coverage across Linux distributions is uneven. As of last week, several still shipped a vulnerable Ghostscript or had a fix pending, according to the firm's survey. The list records package status, not a tested attack path for each system:

Debian 12 (Bookworm): listed as vulnerable

Amazon Linux 2, 2023, and 2027 Preview: fix pending

SUSE Linux Enterprise 12 SP5 and parts of 15: fix in progress

SUSE Linux Enterprise Server 16.0 and 16.1: fix in progress

openSUSE Leap 16.0 and 16.1: affected

The firm did not establish whether current Red Hat, Rocky Linux, AlmaLinux, Oracle Linux, or stable Fedora packages carry the fix.

Until the renderer is updated, the exposure can be reduced by requiring authentication on print queues and by limiting which file types an image processor accepts. System controls help too, and in one test an AppArmor profile stopped the compromised interpreter from starting a shell.

One published proof-of-concept file, described in its technical analysis, shows only the core memory trick. It changes a value inside the running interpreter and sets it back, without running a shell command or touching files on the host, and on the patched 10.08.0 build it fails before that point.

The firm's broader research extended to production services, including Google, which fixed the affected path last week. Its reports of similar problems at Dropbox and other unnamed services are separate Ghostscript bugs, not this one.

A malicious webpage could extract a Meta Muse user's private conversations and stored memories from the assistant without triggering any approval prompt, researchers have shown.

The same attack could be extended to reach the credentials Muse stores for the user's connected accounts.

The work was a laboratory demonstration, not an attack on real users, and it stayed inside the individual user's cloud machine. NebuSec, the security firm that published it yesterday, says Muse's team has since fixed every weakness it describes.

Muse is Meta's personal AI agent, launched on September 8, that works through the online accounts a user connects to it and can make purchases on their behalf. Each person's agent runs on its own cloud computer.

There, a monitoring layer called Sentinel is meant to be the single gate for anything the agent sends out, and the passwords and tokens for connected accounts are supposed to stay beyond the agent's reach.

Muse's team described the aim at launch: "So we designed the system to assume the agent may be under attack and limit the potential damage."

In the demonstration, a malicious page the agent opened could pull a user's conversation history and stored memories to an external server and alter them, with no prompt shown to the user, according to the firm's research write-up.

From there, the researchers chained a flaw in Muse's built-in browser, which the assistant uses for tasks such as creating PDFs, to a Linux kernel flaw, thereby taking full control of the user's cloud machine. They set out that chain in a technical breakdown.

That full control reached the credential store, the saved files, and Sentinel itself, the parts Muse's security design keeps walled off from the agent.

The compromise went no further than that one machine. The researchers did not break out to the shared server beneath it or into other users' machines.

Muse was built to assume its own agent could be turned against the user and to box in any break-in. Each of those controls worked as designed, the firm said, yet the attack still ran the whole way through.

The firm says it reported the findings to Muse's team and that the exploits were all fixed before publication. Meta has not publicly confirmed the fixes or commented on the work, and the write-up does not say whether any user data was accessed before the patches went in.

The firm's Muse research broke into the cloud system behind the assistant, unlike an earlier Muse flaw found in September, which only let malware already on a user's Mac hijack the assistant on the device.

[4] The Hacker News - GnuPG Warns Critical GPGME Flaw Could Expose App Data via Malicious OpenPGP Keys
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Tarih: Sat, 21 Nov 2026 16:35:53 +0530
Link: https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/gnupg-warns-critical-gpgme-flaw-could.html

[TAM METİN - 439 kelime]
A malicious OpenPGP key with an unusually long user ID can trigger a critical memory bug in GPGME, the standard library that programs use to work with GnuPG.

The GnuPG project warned yesterday that the flaw could be used to steal data from affected applications and has released fixed versions.

The bug, tracked as CVE-2026-129975, affects all software that uses GPGME before version 2.3.0, whether the library is loaded as a shared library, a Windows DLL, or statically linked into the program.

Examples the project gives include most mail clients, many XMPP clients, Samba, and its own Gpg4win, GnuPG Desktop, and GnuPG VS-Desktop packages.

The project has not reported any attacks exploiting the flaw, and it notes that remote code execution cannot be ruled out.

Its example of a path to the bug is signed data sent to an application. Which programs pass such material to the library without a manual key import is not something the advisory says.

The flaw is a double free, meaning the same memory is released twice. It sits in two routines, read_status() and read_colon_line(), that read status lines and key listings coming back from GnuPG.

When the input runs long, the routines move the data to a larger buffer but record the new address only if they finish without errors. An error in between leaves a pointer to memory that was already freed, and cleanup then frees it a second time, as the project's tracker entry details.

The flaw was found by Johan Carlsson and reported to the project by GitLab last week. Disclosure was set for November 25, the advisory says, and moved up because the new Ruby2Shell bug forced the company to update earlier.

The fixed library version is GPGME 2.3.1, released this week. A version 2.3.0 was never publicly available.

What to install depends on where the library sits:

Shared library or DLL: update to GPGME 2.3.1, available from the project's official downloads and detailed in its security advisory.

Windows: Gpg4win 5.1.2 bundles the fixed library. Its release notes call the issue severe and say to update as soon as possible.

Older versions that cannot move to 2.3.1: the advisory carries a patch, and the legacy branch has its own fixed release, 1.24.4, whose legacy release notes say the project cannot guarantee that the branch will see every security patch.

Bundled and statically linked copies: these are affected too, and updates to the system's shared library do not reach them, so the applications that contain them need their own rebuilt releases.

GPGMEPP and GPGMEQT: no update is needed.

The advisory offers no interim workaround for software that cannot update yet.

[5] The Hacker News - Attackers' AI Agents Found a Zero-Day in Medical-Imaging Software and Gained Root Access
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Tarih: Sat, 21 Nov 2026 16:33:17 +0530
Link: https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/attackers-ai-agents-found-zero-day-in.html

[TAM METİN - 799 kelime]
Suspected China-linked hackers used autonomous AI agents to find a previously unknown flaw in MONAI Label, an open-source medical imaging server, and gain root access to a university server, Amazon Threat Intelligence said this week.

The flaw allows an attacker with no account to write files outside the server's storage folder, and it affects MONAI Label 0.8.5 and earlier in the default setup, which runs with no login enabled.

The maintainers have merged a fix in the source code, but there is still no fixed version to install.

MONAI Label is a server that research teams use to label medical scans and train imaging models; it is a separate program from the broader MONAI framework.

The weakness is a path-traversal flaw in the parts of the server that store uploaded images and labels. A file identifier supplied by the caller is appended to the storage folder and written to disk without checking that the result remains within that folder.

The server writes with the privileges of the account it runs under, so an attacker cannot write anywhere on the machine, but a writable code or settings location can open a path to running code. Authentication is off by default, so any client that can reach the server over the network can set off the write. Versions 0.8.5 and earlier are affected.

Amazon reconstructed the intrusion after finding the operator's own Cairn control panel sitting on the internet without a password. Cairn is an open-source framework that runs AI agents as automated penetration testers, and the exposed panel revealed the agents' task settings, prompts, and run history.

Over about 48 hours in September, the agents mapped the application's interface and reached the point of writing files to the server in roughly 11 minutes, the company said in its incident report.

The agents worked with little step-by-step direction. The operator set the goal of getting shell access and chose the targets, and the agents selected the methods themselves, according to the company.

Investigators tie the campaign to a China-linked actor with moderate confidence, pointing to Simplified Chinese prompts, China Standard Time settings, and targets likely of interest to China. They did not connect the work to a named group or establish that a government was behind it.

A root shell came later, and not within those 11 minutes. In a separate session, the agents used the same flaw to plant a file that Python runs automatically at startup. They then triggered a training feature that started a new Python process, which loaded that file and connected back to the attacker as root.

That root access was on one university server, and whether another MONAI Label system would give the same result depends on how it is run and what its service account can do.

In a later session, the operator handed the agents a tunnel into a second university network running the same software. Amazon believes the network was broken into in the same way but has not confirmed the second compromise.

The maintainers confirmed the flaw and merged the source fix on October 19, which forces uploads to resolve inside the approved folders and rejects empty names or names that begin with a dot.

Their security advisory, published earlier this week, still lists no patched version and says the correction will arrive in a future release. The newest release listing remains 0.8.5, which dates to 2024. A merged fix in source code is not the same as a package you can install.

The flaw is now listed in the CVE record as CVE-2026-133968, published this week and rated 9.8 out of 10, a critical score. The project's advisory still shows no CVE assigned, which is a lag in updating that page, not a sign of a second flaw.

With no fixed release available, the practical steps are to cut off exposure and limit what a compromised server could reach:

Block untrusted network access to MONAI Label servers with firewall and routing rules, not just hard-to-guess URLs.

Turn on authentication, which the server leaves off by default.

Run the server under a limited account rather than root, and restrict the folders it can write to.

Do not assume a source build is safe. Confirm it includes the October fix before treating it as patched.

If a server was reachable, check for unexpected Python startup files and unusual training jobs, the path the reported attack used.

The company's account is based on its own reconstruction and has not been independently confirmed. It does not show that patient scans or records were taken, that clinical systems were touched, or that the attacker reached past the university targets described.

The campaign also included failed operations, and the root result on one server should not be taken as the outcome for every MONAI Label deployment.

Signal has announced that it will restrict screenshots by default to stop artificial intelligence (AI) agents from reading private chats on Android and iOS devices, but conceded that the protection is "incomplete."

The feature applies to agentic systems built into iOS 27 and on Android versions where the encrypted messaging platform can detect an AI assistant attempting to read the Signal screen.

It also builds upon a similar setting that Signal introduced in Windows 11 last May following Microsoft's release of the controversial Recall feature.

Calling the agentic integrations into Android, iOS, and Windows an "existential threat to Signal," Meredith Whittaker, president of the Signal Foundation, said the move is designed to uphold its privacy guarantees in the AI era.

"In order to gain access to information about you, these OS AI agents 'scan' the information that's on your screen," Whittaker said. "In doing so, your Signal data is captured, then made available to these agentic systems in order to allow them to answer your questions and do your bidding."

"The net result is that these AI capabilities in the operating system are undermining Signal's ability to independently guarantee privacy."

The risk here is that AI agents can capture and store the information accessed from Signal either locally or on remote servers, with the activity occurring outside of the platform's control and potentially putting the privacy of all the participants in a conversation at risk.

Users who want to disable the option can do so by navigating to Settings > Privacy > App Security > toggle "Block Siri AI" (on iOS) or "Screen security" (on Android).

"However, doing so will potentially expose your (and your contacts') Signal content to these AI agents, and break our privacy guarantees," Signal said.

"Our remedy is also incomplete – while we are doing everything we can, restricting screenshots does not address other more sneaky ways AI agents could potentially access application data, such as retrieving content already present on a user's device via the file system."

Signal also pointed out that application developers lack robust tools to prevent AI agents from eroding application-level privacy. It also called out the companies for failing to provide adequate documentation about how these systems work to help design necessary controls to prevent the agents from accessing sensitive application data.

Specifically, it's urging the tech giants to provide "two common-sense remedies" to help protect the privacy of its users -

Access to easy and well-documented way to opt their app out of AI data access and processing entirely, including screen reading, filesystem access, notifications, APIs, and other OS frameworks, without making drastic compromises to the core user experience

Create better documentation and technical specifications around how AI agents function, specifically with regards to data access and processing

The concerns are not theoretical. Reuters recently reported that Muse, Meta's AI agent, was able to bypass its own official "Connector" tool to obtain access to Signal messages on both an Apple computer and an Android device by taking advantage of the broad operating system permissions granted to it during initial setup.

On macOS, for example, the agent leveraged macOS's accessibility and screenshot tools to navigate to the Signal app, take screenshots, extract text from those images, and regenerate the textual content within the Muse app. On Android, it read messages by abusing its access to the device's push notifications.

In one case highlighted by the news agency, the AI agent alerted a Meta staffer using Muse on her personal phone with a reminder to respond to a message from her sister on Signal. "This seems like an extreme privacy violation. I definitely did not grant it access to other apps, or recall being asked about this," the employee said in an internal post.

"We also see a growing need for tools that can prevent third-party agents, like Meta's Muse, from accessing application data using the deep permissions that users are encouraged to grant them during setup," Whittaker said.

"In many cases these agents are leveraging accessibility settings that were meant to empower actual people to get the most out of their apps and services, not to act as a skeleton key that provides access to AI agents."

Late last month, Apple released iOS 27.2 with an "Excluded Apps" option that lets users choose which apps Siri, Search, and suggestions can access. With Google Gemini, users have the ability to disconnect apps via the Connected Apps settings.

Earlier this week, the iPhone maker also previewed new controls that allow Mac users to give more granular access to data they give to apps, including those that use artificial intelligence (AI) agents.

Chinese hackers exploited VMware zero-day

Armory Castle is a cyberespionage group known for exploiting zero-day vulnerabilities in enterprise network appliances, including Citrix NetScaler and Ivanti Connect Secure devices, to breach corporate networks and deploy custom malware.

After gaining access to an internal Linux appliance, the hackers deployed the "SLIMSAVAGE" web shells to maintain persistent access, escalated privileges to root, and scanned the internal network for VMware systems.

The attackers then used a custom Golang exploitation tool named BONESAW to exploit CVE-2026-123890 and compromise a VMware vCenter Server.

Mandiant says the use of two zero-day vulnerabilities in the same campaign, along with multiple malware families tailored to the targeted platforms, demonstrates the threat actor's technical expertise.

Cairn AI Framework Used in Autonomous Zero-Day Exploitation

Amazon uncovered AI-powered attacks on universities, where autonomous agents found a zero-day and gained root access without human guidance.

Amazon’s threat intelligence team found a Chinese-nexus actor running Cairn, an open-source AI pentesting framework, with one instruction for the agents: obtain shell access. No human chose the next step after that. The agents planned, executed, and adjusted on their own, and along the way they found a real zero-day in an open-source medical imaging application.

The discovery happened because the operator left their own server exposed. On 10 September, Amazon spotted a publicly hosted Cairn instance running with no authentication, which meant anyone could see the project configurations, the prompts, and the AI’s output. The prompts were in Simplified Chinese, and script timestamps matched China Standard Time, over a roughly 48-hour campaign against university IP addresses in East Asia, outside mainland China.

“On September 10, 2026, Amazon Threat Intelligence identified a server with a publicly hosted instance of Cairn, an open-source autonomous AI penetration-testing framework, running without authentication. Because the operator left the server publicly accessible, the operational history of the platform was visible to anyone on the internet, including project configurations, actor prompts, and AI output. The operator wrote prompts in Simplified Chinese, and the timestamps in executed scripts were configured to China Standard Time.” reads the report published by Amazon. “Analysis of the instance revealed a campaign targeting IP addresses associated with universities in East Asia outside mainland China over an approximately 48-hour period beginning September 8, 2026.”

Not all targets were vulnerable. At one university, the agents found a Redis server that did not require a password. They spent about 16 hours trying 148 different actions to break in. These included using Redis replication to load code, bypassing its security protections, changing configuration files, damaging memory, and exploiting Log4Shell in a nearby service. When these attempts failed, they tried another approach. They exploited an SSRF vulnerability in a Qdrant server on the same network to explore internal systems. They then tried different passwords against PostgreSQL, SSH, and other services. None of these attempts succeeded, but the AI agents kept changing their tactics and trying new approaches.

In another attack, the agents targeted a university GPU server for more than six hours. They collected about 80 real usernames from university websites, faculty directories, research lab pages, archived websites, and academic papers. They then tried more than 30,000 username and password combinations against SSH and other systems, including a VPN server and the university portal. These attempts also failed to gain access.

The medical imaging target is where it worked. The agents mapped the application’s framework and API endpoints, found an upload parameter, tested it for path traversal, and had it: a working file-write vulnerability. From first mapping the API to writing arbitrary files took about 11 minutes.

“Against a target running an open-source medical imaging AI application, the outcome was different. The agents identified the application’s framework, mapped its API endpoints, and found an upload endpoint with a filename parameter.” continues the report. “They tested that parameter for path traversal, and the parameter was vulnerable. In about 11 minutes, the agents went from mapping the application’s API to arbitrarily writing files on the server.”

A later session turned that access into something worse. The agents wrote a Python reverse shell to sitecustomize.py, a file Python loads automatically on startup, then triggered a training endpoint that spawned a new process and imported the payload. That gave them a root shell with no human picking each step. Amazon assesses with high confidence this was a genuine zero-day, since no fix, advisory, or prior mention existed anywhere for the affected project.

The human operator came back once the agents had proven the bug worked. They handed the agents a proxy tunnel into a second university’s network, where a server ran the same vulnerable software, and told them to use that foothold to reach other machines. Amazon infers the actor had already used the same zero-day to set up that tunnel, which means one AI-found bug became the key to a second network.

Amazon notified the project’s maintainers, who shipped a fix and a security advisory. The affected universities weren’t AWS customers, so Amazon routed the warning through a government partner instead of reaching them directly. Separately, researchers at Gambit Security documented a different, financially motivated actor using the same framework against online retailers, so this isn’t a one-off experiment confined to espionage.

The speed of these operations is the most important point. An AI agent that can find and exploit a zero-day in just a few minutes can move faster than most security teams can investigate a single alert. Even when the attacks failed, the agents kept working for 16 hours without breaks or time limits.

Amazon uses honeypots and network traffic analysis to automatically detect some of this activity at no extra cost. The wider lesson is that defenders need to focus on reliable threat detection and fast responses.

“To keep pace, organizations should prioritize high-confidence detections that trigger automated response, with humans overseeing outcomes rather than reviewing every individual action.” concludes the report.

Sworn in as DOW’s CIO in December 2025, Davies will replace Sean Cairncross, who was confirmed as the top cybersecurity official in August 2025, more than five months after being nominated for the position.



Before her DOW tenure, Davies led cybersecurity at multiple organizations. She served as CISO at Unilever, The Estée Lauder Companies Inc., Hewlett-Packard, and Siemens, and as CSO at Barclays, and founded and was the CEO of the Institute for Cyber.



“Our nation stands in the crossroad of analog, digital, and quantum technologies, our American way of living increasingly interconnected. “The Department congratulates Kirsten Davies on her new role as the National Cyber Director. Throughout her tenure at DOW, she has proven herself to be a dedicated patriot and professional, and we are confident she will advance President Trump’s America First agenda across the cybersecurity landscape,” Secretary of War Pete Hegseth said in an X post.

Amazon: China-Linked Hackers’ AI Agents Exploited Medical Software Zero-Day

A threat actor used autonomous Cairn AI agents to discover and exploit a critical MONAI Label vulnerability.

Amazon says autonomous AI agents run by a China-linked threat actor found and exploited a zero-day vulnerability in MONAI Label, an open source tool designed for AI-assisted annotation of medical images.
The tech giant’s threat intelligence team detected the activity in September, after coming across an exposed instance of Cairn, an open source AI penetration testing framework. The instance had no authentication, so anyone could see the operator’s prompts and the agents’ output.



The records showed a roughly 48-hour campaign against IP addresses belonging to universities in East Asia outside mainland China. The operator gave the agents a list of targets and told them to obtain shell access.



After identifying the software on one target and mapping its API, the agents discovered a path traversal bug in a file upload function.



“In about 11 minutes, the agents went from mapping the application’s API to arbitrarily writing files on the server,” Amazon said.



In a later session, the agents used the flaw to plant a Python reverse shell in a file Python runs every time the interpreter starts, ultimately achieving a root shell.Advertisement. Scroll to continue reading.


“Amazon Threat Intelligence assesses with high confidence that it was a zero-day based on the absence of any corresponding fix, issue, advisory, or prior mention in vulnerability databases for the affected open-source project,” the company said.



The operator then gave the agents a proxy that tunneled into a second university and instructed them to target other devices on its network.



“The server that the proxy connected to was running the same vulnerable software, and we infer the actor had used the zero-day to compromise it and establish the tunnel,” Amazon noted.



The MONAI Label developers published an advisory this week. It rates the bug critical and says it affects versions 0.8.5 and earlier. Authentication is off by default, enabling anyone who can reach the server over the network to exploit the vulnerability. A fix has been created and will be included in the next release. 



Amazon has attributed the attack to an unidentified Chinese-nexus threat actor with moderate confidence, based on the language used in the prompts, the time zone in the scripts, and the fact that the targeted universities are likely of strategic interest to China.
