[1] The Hacker News - AI-Assisted Cyberattacks Trigger Unauthorized Payments at Indian Banks and Fintech Firms
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Tarih: Mon, 23 Nov 2026 13:54:15 +0530
Link: https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/ai-assisted-cyberattacks-trigger.html

[TAM METİN - 2325 kelime]
The Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In) has warned that threat actors are carrying out artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted cyber attacks targeting information and communication technology (ICT) assets in the country.

This unsanctioned activity has resulted in unauthorized financial transactions at banking and financial services entities, as well as technology service providers supporting government-linked payments and platforms handling vendor or partner payouts.

"In the observed incidents, attackers targeted business records and approval processes to carry out unauthorized transactions through otherwise legitimate systems," CERT-In said in an advisory last week.

The incidents have been found to include the following methods -

Abuse of business applications and service provider-operated workflows, as opposed to direct exploitation of payment-related APIs.

Modification of business records or approval relationships, followed by the use of legitimate payment processes.

Use of trusted systems and established approval procedures to process unauthorized transactions without raising any alert.

Exploitation of security blind spots in independent verification, reconciliation, and review of changes to business records..

CERT-In did not reveal how much money had been improperly paid out, or by whom. The activity has not been attributed to any specific threat actor.

The agency also said that separate threat-hunting has uncovered the use of AI-driven agentic offensive frameworks (AOFS) across enterprise, technology, government, academic, and research sectors to conduct the following actions -

Automated reconnaissance to identify exposed systems, services, and potential attack paths.

Rapid generation and modification of exploits for known vulnerabilities, including by analyzing software patches and related technical information.

Using a cluster of AI agents to undertake intrusion tasks, such as reconnaissance and privilege discovery, in parallel.

Modifying scripts, requests, and attack methods in response to errors, blocked attempts, or information returned by targeted systems.

Using valid credentials, legitimate administrative functions, trust relationships, and external infrastructure to burrow deeper into target networks.

"Organizations should review the security of business applications, critical records, user identities and third-party interfaces," CERT-In said. "Existing monitoring and incident-response arrangements should also account for the risks arising from misuse of trusted business processes and automated intrusion activity."

The development comes as Indian entities have witnessed a spate of AI-assisted cyber attacks on financial entities, even as it disclosed that AI agents under research and evaluation "exceeded their task authorization and made unauthorized access" to ICT assets.

"In the financially motivated campaign against Indian entities, agentic tooling compressed the time between initial access and final impact from days to a few hours," CERT-In said. "In addition, the campaign highlighted that multi-agent operations run in parallel for hours to days to achieve the attacker goals, consistent with the documented multi-agent capability of these tools."

Earlier this month, the agency also warned that a threat group named Crafty Falcon is behind a wave of AI-driven attacks on financial ​firms and has used offensive penetration testing tools like ARTEX, PentAGI, CAIRN, and CyberStrikeAI to automate the intrusions at machine speed.

According to the Indian Cybercrime Coordination Centre (I4C), the attacks have targeted banks, non-banking financial companies, and fintech companies, leading to multi-crore (equivalent to 10 million) unauthorized transactions through fund disbursement systems and APIs.

"Perpetrators exploit unpatched software or misconfigured internet-exposed servers / IT systems to compromise the infrastructure," I4C said. "Hackers then target automated fund disbursement systems or special interfaces (APIs) used for payments and fund transfers.  The money is sent to 'mule' accounts (accounts used by criminals) across different banks and then quickly moved or withdrawn through IMPS/NEFT."

India's central bank, the Reserve Bank of India, has also alerted of rising cyber attacks on financial infrastructure, urging banks to provide details of their ability to stay ahead of AI-related threats, Reuters reported late last month.

The Surge in AI Attacks

The advisories also come at a time when organizations around the world are being targeted using AI tools, giving attackers a way to "execute multi-stage intrusions at greater operational velocity." Last week, the European Commission confirmed that attackers armed with ARTEX broke into its HR, medical reimbursement, and translation systems. The first signs of the attack were detected on October 22, 2026.

ARTEX has also been implicated in a recent supply chain attack on IT management software company ConnectWise and Barracuda, with the latter revealing that a financially motivated criminal weaponized an exposed valid cloud access key provided to a third-party vendor to map its reach, repurpose discovered secrets to access more systems and look for seed phrases, and exfiltrate more credentials and secrets.

"In all, the attacker touched about 10 cloud accounts in roughly 44 hours [between November 1 and 2]. The work was methodical and tireless," Barracuda said. "The probe names, the other indicators we collected, the order in which the attacker worked through our systems, and its built-in routines for hunting credentials and wallets all match ARTEX."

Frameworks like ARTEX manage a collection of sub-agents to plan, execute, and adapt tactics across multiple kill chain stages, including reconnaissance and scanning, initial access, persistence and privilege escalation, internal reconnaissance, lateral movement, and data exfiltration.

They can translate tactical goals described in natural language into machine commands that are executed by specialized AI agents. It integrates with various external LLMs, including Anthropic Claude, OpenAI GPT, DeepSeek, SpaceXAI Grok, Zhipu GLM, and Kimi.

"ARTEX significantly reduces the time required to chain attack techniques, with some intrusions progressing from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours," the U.S. Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) said in an alert this month.

"The framework enables threat actors with minimal technical expertise to conduct complex, multi-target operations simultaneously, fundamentally accelerating attack velocity and expanding operational scale."

Check Point, in a report published this month, said it uncovered more than 1,800 publicly exposed instances for ARTEX, with attackers leveraging the tool to conduct opportunistic, financially-motivated cybercrime.

"Analysis of agent conversations revealed that these systems work almost fully autonomously, once the initial goal is set: most actors barely interacted with their agent fleets and mostly asked for progress or directed the agents to a new target," the company said.

Data security firm Cohesity said threat actors using ARTEX are gaining access to systems that receive less security attention, such as partner and supplier portals, mobile backends, employee and sales support applications, legacy services, third-party integrations, and applications built by individual business units.

"In a research network we operate, a threat actor linked to Rhysida used an AI framework to rewrite tools in response to obstacles, often within minutes, and more than 150 versions of a single script reached a domain controller within a day," it said.

In a report published last week, Google's Mandiant and Threat Intelligence Group (GTIG) identified what it described as the first observed instance of attackers using an autonomous AI framework with no human in the loop to turn security patches into exploits.

The AI framework, called "exploit_agent," integrates with multiple frontier and local large language models (LLMs). It's designed to discover software vulnerabilities and execute exploits without human intervention. The attacker, suspected to be Russian-speaking, is said to have leveraged the framework to target French websites associated with the media and entertainment industries, as well as the education sector.

"The framework is set up to conduct continuous differential analysis of popular web application software," Mandiant and GTIG said. "It compares code before and after patch announcements, analyzes findings to identify potential vulnerabilities, drafts exploits, and automatically executes them without human intervention."

Some of the other notable incidents where AI agents and models do the legwork are listed below -

Use of CAIRN in intrusion activity against targets, including in the aerospace sector in the U.S.

Embedding LLMs into influence operation workflows to produce synthetic media, create convincing virtual personas, and generate and publish content.

Use of Google's Gemini API, along with tools from multiple AI providers, to build an end-to-end influence-as-a-service (SaaS) platform designed to target public sentiment on regional political topics, specifically across Europe and the Middle East.

Use of LLMs by a China-linked contractor called Leaky Castle to analyze, translate, and understand exfiltrated data

Abuse of a frontier model's file-hosting infrastructure by a China-nexus threat actor to stage lure archives and exfiltrate stolen data, and a backend API to exfiltrate stolen intranet documents.

Use of LLMs by North Korean threat actors to generate resumes and prepare for job interviews at Western organizations.

Creation of fraudulent recruiter profiles on professional networking platforms by a suspected North Korean hacking group called Midnight Neptune (previously UNC1069) in attacks aimed at cryptocurrency entities in North America and Europe using job- and ClickFix-themed lures. These personas have been found to direct targets to fraudulent meeting platforms, which are used to run diagnostic fix scripts via ClickFix. "Following initial compromise, the actor pivoted into internal code repositories to execute a supply chain vector internal to the organization: modifying .claude/settings.json configuration files so that when users started up Claude Code, it would automatically trigger the download of the SOMBERMEME backdoor," Google said.

Use of CyberStrikeAI ("43.240.29[.]11") between September 30 and October 16, 2026, to validate stolen AWS credentials, harvest secrets from Secrets Manager, pull EC2 user data, decrypt an SSM parameter, and send a remote command. "The audit log does not show what the command was or whether it succeeded," Elastic Security Labs said.

Use of Hermes, in combination with DeepSeek, by a threat actor called Silent Lynx in cyber espionage operations targeting Central Asian governments and critical infrastructure from April to October 2026 to assist with reconnaissance, data collection, and erasing traces of activity from compromised systems.

Use of ARTEX to exploit a vulnerable custom PHP upload function and stage a web shell, and use it to access secrets and API keys, and ultimately compromise a company's development pipeline to alter a Git repository and deploy an attacker-controlled Kubernetes pod.

Use of an AI coding assistant to revive a stalled attack at a U.S. retail organization and come up with ways to bypass defenses and steal credentials after their commands were blocked by security software in late September 2026. The attack is assessed to be financially motivated, based on the threat actor's scans for passwords in point-of-sale scripts and an IP address overlap with a prior campaign targeting online retailers.

Use of CAIRN by a Chinese-speaking attacker between August and September 2026 and pairing it with GLM and DeepSeek models to autonomously conduct 25 operations against at least 19 organizations. The attacks, attributed to CL-STA-1238, have targeted advanced semiconductor manufacturing, U.S. defense policy, and civil society sectors, achieving unauthorized access or data retrieval at 10 of the entities, including valid credentials, personal data, military and government identities, and the creation of persistent administrator accounts in a production system. "Cairn coordinates its activities through a shared 'blackboard," Palo Alto Networks Unit 42 said. "Agents read the blackboard and write their findings back as a fact-intent graph, while human operators can inject 'hints' at any time to help guide the operations if needed. Confirmed findings accumulate as immutable facts, and the agent queues its next actions as intents."

Use of ARTEX to gain access to a compromised account-level API token previously used for CI/CD with no IP restrictions or rate limiting. "Attackers repeatedly compromised static, non-expiring tokens running inside CI/CD pipelines, which granted broad, cross-environment access across the organization's hyperscaler infrastructure," Cloudflare said. "LLM frameworks greatly expand the likelihood of a client’s API key being laterally leveraged to take over other tenant environments."

Use of ARTEX to generate deepfake videos to trick identity verification checks at two cryptocurrency exchanges and steal AI credentials at "industrial scale" to gain free access to models. Amazon has characterized the second campaign as a "self-sustaining operation in which stolen keys paid for the LLM inference powering further credential hunting." Targets include LLM gateways and proxy platforms such as One API, New API, Veloera, Sub2API, and LiteLLM, and AI application builders such as Dify, FastGPT, Langflow (CVE-2025-3248), RAGFlow (CVE-2026-28797), Open WebUI, LobeChat, and Ollama.

Use of CAIRN by a suspected Chinese-nexus threat actor to discover a zero-day vulnerability (GHSA-qc22-rmgc-vg94) in MONAI Label, a medical imaging application, and use it to gain complete control of a server within 11 minutes. The activity was detected in September 2026.

"An agent that discovers and exploits a zero-day in minutes completes its activity faster than many security operations centers can manually triage an alert," CJ Moses, CISO of Amazon Integrated Security, said in a report shared with The Hacker News.

"The most extensive operation targeted a Redis (remote dictionary server) instance at one university that was configured to allow access without authentication. The agents worked through a long list of known techniques for taking control of an open Redis server. The agents that failed against the hardened Redis target ran continuously for 16 hours without breaks, sleep, or working-hour constraints."

What's notable about accelerating AI-enabled attacks is that the technology is not just used as a force multiplier but has also emerged as the target.

"The integration of AI-enabled tools into enterprise workflows is creating new attack surfaces, particularly where those tools hold privileged access to code, credentials, data, or connected applications," Recorded Future said. "Developer tools are a primary target of attacks on AI systems. Threat actors exploited weak access controls and software supply-chain vulnerabilities to steal data or establish persistence."

Besides automating the time-consuming phases of intrusions, LLM-driven pentesting frameworks also lower the technical barrier to entry, enabling actors with minimal expertise to carry out sophisticated attacks.

"Now, an attacker can give an agent a goal in plain language," GitGuardian said. "The agent can investigate the environment, select tools, execute commands, interpret the results, and decide what to try next. The tools are also becoming better at recovering from failed attempts. An agent can encounter an error, interpret the response, modify its next command, and continue investigating."

"Anyone with access to AntiFox or ARTEX can just verbally say into the dictation, 'find exposed credentials, determine what they access, and identify valuable resources.' That incantation initiates a series of technical actions that previously required considerably more hands-on expertise."

[TAM METİN - 546 kelime]
​The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) warned federal agencies that attackers are now exploiting a recently patched critical Cisco Expressway vulnerability in the wild.

Cisco Expressway Series devices are enterprise collaboration gateways (deployed as virtual machines or physical hardware appliances) that connect internal voice and video systems with outside networks and remote users via a secure connection.

Tracked as CVE-2026-76466, this security flaw stems from the use of hard-coded credentials and a failure to fully validate client certificates, enabling unauthenticated attackers to remotely execute arbitrary operating system commands as root on affected Cisco Expressway-C and Cisco Expressway-E devices configured in a cluster.

While Cisco says that its Product Security Incident Response Team (PSIRT) is not aware of active exploitation in the wild or publicly available proof-of-concept exploits targeting this vulnerability, CISA tagged it as actively exploited on Friday.

The cybersecurity agency also added it to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) Catalog on Tuesday and ordered US Federal Civilian Executive Branch (FCEB) agencies to secure their systems within three days, as mandated by Binding Operational Directive (BOD) 26-04.

Although BOD 26-04 targets only federal agencies, CISA urged all network defenders, including private-sector security teams, to prioritize patching this flaw to block ongoing attacks that may target their networks.

"These types of vulnerabilities are a frequent attack vector for malicious cyber actors and pose significant risks to the federal enterprise," it warned.

Exploited to gain root on Internet-exposed devices

While CISA has yet to share details on these ongoing attacks, Russian cybersecurity company F6 said on Thursday that it spotted attackers using an unnamed AI assistant to successfully exploit CVE-2026-7646 and gain remote command execution with full root privileges on Internet-exposed Cisco Expressway devices.

"The attackers initially compromised two Cisco Expressway internet-facing devices. Through one of them, they gained access to two additional cluster nodes located on the organization’s internal network," F6 security researchers noted.

"After obtaining root access, the attackers began preparing tooling for further lateral movement across the infrastructure. They used custom Python scripts designed to scan the network, discover accessible SMB resources, interact with SQL Server and WinRM, and explore GitLab repositories in order to exfiltrate credentials and other sensitive information."

Two weeks ago, CISA also flagged a high-severity Cisco BroadWorks vulnerability (CVE-2026-20320) as actively exploited in attacks and ordered federal agencies to patch their systems by November 24.

According to threat intelligence firm Malbear Labs, a Chinese-speaking threat actor abused CVE-2026-20320 in autonomous AI attacks targeting a European telecommunications provider and 13 organizations that used its data centers and cloud infrastructure.

CISA has added 103 Cisco vulnerabilities to its list of actively exploited flaws since November 2021, seven of which ransomware gangs have also used in the wild.

[3] BleepingComputer - Hackers exploit GL.iNet cloud flaw to infect routers, KVM devices
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Tarih: Sun, 22 Nov 2026 13:45:37 -0500
Link: https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/hackers-exploit-glinet-cloud-flaw-to-infect-routers-kvm-devices/

[TAM METİN - 1196 kelime]
GL.iNet has confirmed that hackers exploited a vulnerability in its cloud remote management services to install custom malware on customers' routers and KVM devices, with some affected users reporting backdoors and reverse shells giving remote access to infected devices.

GL.iNet manufactures networking equipment, including home and travel routers, as well as KVM devices that allow users to access and control computers remotely.

Its GoodCloud platform allows customers to remotely manage supported devices, including accessing and controlling computers and servers connected to its KVM products.

In a security advisory published on November 20, GL.iNet confirmed that an unauthorized party exploited a vulnerability in its cloud remote management services to access devices and install malicious software.

"The attack path has been blocked. We fixed the cloud vulnerability and blocked the access used in this incident," the company said.

"Devices can no longer be newly infected through this attack path. These measures do not remove malware from devices that were already infected."

GL.iNet has not disclosed how the attackers exploited its cloud platform, how many customers were impacted, or whether information was stolen from compromised devices.

The company is also investigating whether computers connected to infected KVM devices were compromised.

GL.iNet recommends that customers check their devices for signs of infection and install the latest security firmware, even if no malware is detected.

The company's initial instructions told customers to look for a process running as crond -i /tmp/.log, which GL.iNet said was an indicator of compromise.

While the cloud vulnerability has been fixed, GL.iNet says stable firmware updates containing additional security fixes for both routers and KVM devices are scheduled for release on November 22, 2026.

For KVM devices, customers can follow the company's firmware upgrade guidance, which recommends version 1.10.3 or later for the RM10, RM1, RM1V2, RM1PE, and RM4PE, version 1.10.3 beta or later for the RM10RC, and version 1.8.7 or later for the RMQ1.

Router owners should install the latest security firmware for their specific model as soon as it becomes available.

However, GL.iNet forum members warn that the company's detection instructions may not identify all compromised devices, including those infected with other backdoors or running reverse shells.

GL.iNet has linked to a community-developed detection and cleanup tool as part of the company's official support instructions for routers and KVM devices.

The scanner performs additional checks for known malware files, suspicious processes, modified system utilities, and configuration files modified for persistence. It can also collect forensic evidence and optionally remove known malware components.

The project's developers warn that the scanner cannot detect every infection and that compromised devices may require reinstalling firmware without retaining existing settings, as malicious files stored in configuration directories could survive an ordinary firmware upgrade.

Customers who suspect their devices were compromised should preserve evidence before resetting them, avoid restoring potentially infected configurations, and rotate credentials and access keys that may have been exposed.

GL.iNet also asked customers who run the scanner to upload the anonymous report when prompted so the developers can determine the number and types of devices impacted.

For KVM users, the malware may have allowed access to the computers controlled by the KVM, so those devices should also be analyzed for potential compromise.

Malware and reverse shells discovered

Following the disclosure, GL.iNet customers and security researchers shared evidence of custom malware infections designed specifically for GL.iNET devices, as well as root-level reverse shells that connected compromised routers to the IP address 170.39.226[.]219.

The reverse shells used the Linux nc utility to create outbound connections, allowing remote attackers to execute commands on infected devices.

Researchers at RemoteToHome Consulting also identified a potential command injection vulnerability in GL.iNet's GoodCloud client involving a handler used for AstroWarp/SD-WAN functionality.

According to the researchers, the handler passes cloud-supplied values into shell commands executed with root privileges without properly validating them.

However, GL.iNet has not confirmed that this particular vulnerability was used in the attacks.

RemoteToHome says it analyzed 183 routers it owns or manages and found suspicious activity on 152, including 24 with root-level reverse shells, 47 with an older persistent backdoor, and 81 showing evidence that attacker-supplied commands had executed.

The researchers note that their findings may not be the same as the other GL.iNet customers and that the older backdoor infections may not be linked to the November attacks.

In a separate forum report, the owner of a compromised GL-RM1 KVM shared network logs showing it downloading a malicious ni.sh script and additional executable payloads from 66.245.219[.]252 on November 19.

The owner also reported that the device began communicating with approximately 1,800 residential IP addresses daily and repeatedly contacted the TRON blockchain API, exhibiting activity suggestive of a peer-to-peer network.

However, the purpose of these connections is unclear, and there is no confirmation that the compromised devices were being used as residential proxies or were part of a larger botnet.

BleepingComputer examined a copy of the malicious ni.sh script [VirusTotal] and found that it contains code specifically tailored to GL.iNet routers and KVM devices.

The script recognizes numerous GL.iNet device models and downloads malicious executables named gl-helper and gb from hardcoded servers.

It also establishes persistence through startup scripts and scheduled tasks, allowing the malware to restart after a reboot or if its processes are terminated.

The installer includes functionality to disguise the malicious process as crond -i /tmp/.log, matching the IOC published by GL.iNet.

An interesting feature is replacing the legitimate netstat utility with a modified shell script that filters out connections to 66.245.219[.]252, potentially hiding the malware's communications from administrators examining network connections.

The installer also contains functionality to install SSH public keys for remote access, modify file timestamps, and periodically contact a remote server to retrieve and execute commands.

The attacker's infrastructure includes two IP addresses, 66.245.219[.]252 and 65.20.71[.]131, which match the IP addresses seen in the compromised KVM owner's logs.

The script also contains Chinese-language developer comments describing its persistence mechanisms, reporting functionality, and interactions with GL.iNet's cloud management software, indicating a Chinese-speaking individual may have written it.

BleepingComputer also examined a separate malicious Lua payload recovered from a GL-MT2500 router.

The payload was launched through a modified urandom_seed startup script and included functionality to collect device information, contact remote servers, and download and execute additional programs.

Some of the malicious files recovered from GL.iNet devices had timestamps dating to December 2025, suggesting that some devices may have been compromised for months. However, it is unclear whether these infections involved the same threat actors or were related to the November 20 attacks.

GL.iNet says it continues to investigate the incident and plans to notify identified affected customers using the email addresses associated with their cloud accounts.

E-commerce platform Cleverbridge is warning that hackers breached its systems and stole customers' payment card information, including credit card numbers and CVV security codes.

Cleverbridge provides e-commerce services to software and SaaS companies, allowing them to sell products, process payments, and manage subscriptions.

The company lists numerous technology brands as its customers, including Red Hat, SUSE, Tenable, Veeam, Jamf, Sony, and Parallels. However, it has not disclosed which businesses' customers were impacted by the breach.

In a new page about the security incident, Cleverbridge says it detected unauthorized activity on November 4, 2026, and temporarily took systems offline to contain the breach.

"All systems were brought back online on 4 November 2026; however, our investigation subsequently determined that a third party copied payment card data stored on our platform," reads the security incident disclosure.

Cleverbridge began notifying affected customers on November 20, warning that hackers had stolen payment card details used to make purchases through its platform.

While its public disclosure confirms the theft of payment card data, it does not specify which card details were stolen, instead referring affected customers to their individual notifications for more information.

However, a breach notification shared with BleepingComputer reveals that the stolen information includes payment card numbers, expiration dates, and CVV/CVC security codes.

"Based on our investigation to date, the information involved includes the payment card details you used to make a purchase through Cleverbridge, notably your card number, expiry date, card security code (CVV/CVC)," reads the notification.

"Note however that no other personal data (for example your name, contact or billing details) were copied. The affected card ends in XXXX."

BleepingComputer redacted the last four digits of the affected payment card.

The company has not disclosed how the attackers gained access to its platform, how long they remained in its systems, or how many customers were affected by the breach.

Of particular concern is the theft of CVV/CVC security codes, which are used to verify payment card transactions.

Cleverbridge also confirmed that the incident was not a ransomware attack and that attackers did not encrypt any of its systems.

The company says it has notified data protection authorities, law enforcement, payment card networks, and payment partners about the incident.

Cleverbridge is also working with TransUnion's Cyberscout to distribute breach notifications and provide affected customers with complimentary credit monitoring and fraud assistance.

Affected customers are told to review their payment card statements for unauthorized transactions. Cleverbridge also recommends contacting card issuers about the incident, as banks may decide to replace compromised cards.

BleepingComputer contacted Cleverbridge with questions about how many customers were affected, how the attackers accessed its systems, whether the stolen payment card data was encrypted, and why CVV/CVC security codes were accessible.

The company has not responded to our questions.

[5] BleepingComputer - Signal to block Siri, Gemini AI from capturing chats on iOS, Android
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Tarih: Sun, 22 Nov 2026 10:15:25 -0500
Link: https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/signal-to-block-siri-gemini-ai-from-capturing-chats-on-ios-android/

[TAM METİN - 495 kelime]
Signal has announced new protections that block screenshots on iOS 27 and prevent AI assistants from capturing conversations on supported Android devices.

The protections are designed to stop Siri and Gemini, the AI assistants built into iOS and Android, from accessing private conversations while performing tasks for users.

The change comes after Signal introduced a feature in May 2025 that blocks Microsoft's controversial Recall screenshot-capturing feature on Windows 11.

"We will now be taking similar measures to restrict agentic systems built into iOS 27 and on Android versions where we can detect an AI assistant attempting to read your Signal screen," Signal's president, Meredith Whittaker, said in a blog post.

Whittaker says OS-level features such as Siri's Onscreen Awareness and Google's Gemini Intelligence can capture chat content as it appears on the app's screen, putting potentially sensitive private data outside Signal's control and exposing all conversation participants.

Signal says its engineers spent months figuring out how AI systems access app data, designing targeted protections to block all extraction pathways verified through reverse engineering.

However, Signal warns that AI agents could still access conversations through other methods, which Whittaker describes as "an existential threat to Signal."

She explains that AI agents could access conversation data in multiple ways, as they are now embedded deep in operating systems, leaving apps vulnerable and with limited options for protecting users.

The most notable example is macOS, where Signal says it's currently unable to provide any screenshot-blocking protection.

For macOS 27 users, Signal recommends adding the app to the Excluded Apps list under System Settings > Siri to prevent Siri AI from proactively accessing message content.

For this reason, Signal calls on Apple, Google, and Microsoft to fully disclose, through detailed documentation, all potential access mechanisms and give app developers a clear way to opt out of AI access across screens, files, notifications, and APIs.

Signal did not provide specific app version numbers for the new protections but says they are automatically activated when possible.

Signal recommends that users upgrade to the latest available version and go to Settings > App Security to check that Block Siri AI is enabled on iOS.

On Android, Signal says it automatically enables Screen Security when it detects an AI assistant attempting to access the screen.

However, on iOS, the protection also prevents users from taking screenshots of their Signal conversations, even for legitimate purposes.

BADBOX 3.0 Exposed: Massive Botnet Hides Ad Fraud Inside Android TV Boxes

BADBOX 3.0 returns with 40 billion daily ad requests, turning infected TV boxes into fraud machines

The BADBOX botnet is back, and its latest version has reached a scale that makes previous ad fraud operations look relatively small. Researchers from HUMAN’s Satori Threat Intelligence and Research team have uncovered BADBOX 3.0, a large-scale operation that uses infected Android-based TV boxes and other low-cost consumer devices to generate billions of fraudulent advertising requests every day.

“At its height, BADBOX 3.0 accounted for roughly 40 billion bid requests a day and more than 4% of all CTV traffic observed by the Human Defense Platform. BADBOX 3.0’s volume was more than three times the volume of the previous record holder, VASTFLUX, and represents a larger peak than the last five years’ worth of published Satori investigations combined.” reads the report published by Human.

“BADBOX 3.0 runs primarily on low-cost consumer devices infected with new backdoors that deliver fraud modules, including some that HUMAN researchers recognized from BADBOX and BADBOX 2.0.”

According to the investigation, the operation peaked at around 40 billion bid requests per day, accounting for more than 4% of all connected TV (CTV) traffic observed by HUMAN’s Defense Platform. That’s more than three times the volume attributed to VASTFLUX, the previous record holder.

The latest findings show that the operators haven’t simply rebuilt the previous botnet. They’ve expanded its capabilities by combining advertising fraud with the abuse of residential proxy networks, using the same compromised devices for both activities.

“The investigation began with a backdoor packaged as a system application with two hardcoded endpoints.” continues the report. “One endpoint retrieves the list of modules to load in the background, and the second handles logging.”

BADBOX 3.0 primarily targets inexpensive, off-brand Android devices, including TV boxes, smartphones and tablets running the Android Open Source Project (AOSP). Some of these devices reportedly arrive with backdoors already installed, allowing attackers to gain privileged access and install additional malicious components.

The advertising fraud works by running video ads in hidden windows. The infected device plays the ads in the background, while the malware generates advertising requests that impersonate legitimate connected TV applications. Advertisers and advertising platforms therefore receive what appears to be genuine traffic from popular streaming services, even though the applications being impersonated aren’t actually running on the infected devices.

“Modules delivered through BADBOX 3.0 mimic video ad software development kits (SDKs) and render the ads in a hidden window, so the ad plays on the device while the screen shows nothing unusual.” HUMAN researchers report. “The resulting bid requests present themselves as coming from well-known, legitimate CTV apps that the threat actors impersonated to route their traffic. “

The investigation also uncovered another revenue stream. The malware silently enrolls infected devices in residential proxy networks, turning their owners’ internet connections into infrastructure that third parties can rent and use.

Researchers identified 11 such networks, including ten commercially available services and PXYEDGE, a network allegedly operated by the same threat actors behind the BADBOX campaigns.

“One of those networks is PXYEDGE, the rebranded successor to the IpMoYu network exposed in BADBOX 2.0.” the report states. “Not all of the operation’s ad fraud requires an infected device: HUMAN also observed spoofed server-side ad insertion (SSAI) traffic monetized through many of the same supply paths.”

The device owner unknowingly provides the bandwidth and residential IP address, while someone else monetizes that access.

This creates a problem that extends beyond advertising fraud. Residential proxies can help criminals conceal the origin of their activities, making it harder to identify attacks involving account takeovers, fake account creation, payment card fraud and other forms of abuse.

The report also establishes a connection between PXYEDGE and IpMoYu, the residential proxy network previously exposed during the BADBOX 2.0 investigation. Researchers found overlapping infrastructure, proxy modules and task servers, concluding that PXYEDGE is likely IpMoYu operating under a different name. They also observed approximately 340,000 unique connections across monitored services, while the network advertised around 200,000 available nodes.

“The eleventh network is PXYEDGE, operated by the threat actors themselves and described below.” Human states. “Enrolling a device in these networks turns the owner’s home internet connection into a commodity without their knowledge or consent. The device owner pays for the bandwidth, and someone else sells access to the address. “

Another important finding concerns the infrastructure behind the operation. Satori researchers linked the BADBOX 3.0 malware catalog to TXCZ, a Chinese electronics manufacturer, through several independent technical connections.

These include command-and-control infrastructure, manufacturer-owned domains, preinstalled software, device-specific checks embedded in the malware and public code repositories used to distribute malicious modules. Researchers also identified a repository account incorporating TXCZ’s name and linked its maintainer to the threat activity.

These findings establish several connections between the malware infrastructure and the manufacturer. They don’t, by themselves, establish the extent of the company’s knowledge or involvement in the criminal operation.

The operators have also taken steps to make the botnet harder to disrupt. Instead of relying on a single download location, they distribute malicious modules through public code repositories and multiple backup hosting services. Some payloads are mirrored through an open-source content delivery network, while a self-hosted GitHub proxy helps devices retrieve files even when access to the original repositories is blocked.

This approach also gives researchers visibility into the malware’s development. By examining repository histories, Satori identified an actively maintained catalog containing hundreds of module versions, including components associated with earlier BADBOX campaigns and the vo1d malware family.

BADBOX has survived previous disruption efforts. Researchers first discovered the malware in 2023, when they found low-cost Android devices with a backdoor already installed in their firmware. In 2025, they uncovered BADBOX 2.0, a much larger operation that had infected more than 10 million devices across 222 countries and territories. Google, HUMAN, Trend Micro, Shadowserver and other partners then worked together to disrupt it. However, as BleepingComputer reports, the operators have returned with a new version.

HUMAN also joined a separate operation led by Google to disrupt XCPE, a related but different malware campaign. Researchers found more than one million infected devices in over 200 countries and territories. The operation also helped identify and take down several command-and-control domains used by BADBOX 3.0.

The researchers stress that disrupting the infrastructure doesn’t necessarily eliminate the threat. Previous investigations showed that the operators could rebuild their capabilities by moving to new servers, changing distribution methods and adapting their malware.

For consumers, the advice remains straightforward: buy devices from reputable manufacturers, check that Android devices are Play Protect certified, and install applications only through official marketplaces. Cheap, uncertified Android TV boxes deserve particular scrutiny, especially when their software and manufacturer cannot be independently verified.

The most important lesson from BADBOX 3.0 is that a consumer device can become part of a criminal operation without its owner noticing anything unusual. It can generate fraudulent advertising revenue, provide access to a residential internet connection and help conceal other malicious activities. The problem isn’t limited to the money lost by advertisers. It’s also about consumers unknowingly providing the infrastructure criminals need to operate.

“Each chapter of this research has ended with a version of the same observation: disrupting an operation and dismantling the conditions that produced it are different achievements.” the report concludes. “BADBOX 3.0 shows what that intact supply chain produces. The capability now lives in public code repositories, mirrored across hosting platforms, a mainstream CDN, and a proxy built to slip past network blocks, with fallback locations written into every module record. Its proxy network survived exposure by changing its name. Its ad fraud, hidden on the devices and passed off under the names of legitimate apps, reached a daily volume larger than any ad fraud operation disclosed before it.”

The issue affects the XML-RPC endpoint, which accepts and deserializes vendor extension types before authenticating requests. An unauthenticated remote attacker can exploit this behavior to execute arbitrary code on a vulnerable server. The Apache Roller maintainers have confirmed that attackers are exploiting the vulnerability in the wild, and CISA has added it to the KEV catalog. However, neither the project maintainers nor public reports have identified the threat actors behind these attacks or provided details about their targets and objectives.

ARTEX: How Cybercriminals Use AI Agents to Scale Their Attacks

Amazon spotted AI agents running on ARTEX steal AI credentials and test deepfake ID bypasses.

Amazon’s threat intelligence team published findings on criminals running offensive campaigns with ARTEX, an open-source agentic AI penetration testing framework. Between 3 October and 5 November, Amazon’s MadPot network of decoy sensors caught requests from ARTEX agents. Some sensors posed as popular large language model APIs, and the agents sent them their own campaign records.

“The ARTEX agents sent these requests to MadPot sensors configured to present as popular large language model (LLM) inference APIs, and the requests carried the agents’ own records of their campaigns.” reads the report. “Amazon Threat Intelligence assessed threat activity referencing potentially affected AWS customers and notified them as appropriate.”

These records reveal what the attackers were doing. AI agent frameworks save previous conversations, so a single request often contained details of the entire operation, including instructions, tool activity, shared information and results reported by the agents. Amazon did not observe the attacks in real time and could not confirm whether the agents had actually achieved their goals. However, the records showed what the attackers were trying to do.

Amazon believes with moderate confidence that the attackers used ARTEX, based on references to the framework in their prompts and other technical evidence. It also believes with moderate confidence that several separate groups with links to China were involved. The evidence includes prompts written in Simplified Chinese, time-zone settings linked to China, targets matching Chinese interests and tools commonly used by China-linked groups. Some instructions told the agents to avoid targets in mainland China and government domains, and to route all outgoing traffic through proxy servers.

Operators set the mission and left the rest to the agents. Planner, worker, verification and compression agents shared a persistent knowledge graph, so each picked up where the others stopped. In one campaign, operators told agents to work through a fixed list of countries, favor enterprise production sites over personal projects and grade every credential. Keys exposed publicly for more than 30 days counted as low-value inventory.

The biggest campaign stole credentials for AI models, and it paid for itself. Stolen keys went into the framework’s own model gateway and powered the agents hunting for more keys. Agents also validated cloud identities, listed storage, checked permissions and went after managed AI services, secrets stores and application identities, with premium frontier models first.

The agents focused on applications that store API keys from multiple providers. A single poorly protected admin page could expose credentials for many accounts. The targets included API gateways and proxies such as One API, New API, Veloera, Sub2API and LiteLLM, as well as AI development platforms such as Dify, FastGPT, Langflow, RAGFlow, Open WebUI, LobeChat and Ollama.

The framework included a set of instructions for attacking each product. These covered common weaknesses, such as installations that had not been properly configured, exposed admin pages, open provider settings and default passwords, including LiteLLM’s default master key. The agents also looked for known vulnerabilities, including CVE-2025-3248 in Langflow and CVE-2026-28797 in RAGFlow. Other techniques included bypassing authentication in One API-related systems by abusing the isCliRequest header, exploiting server-side request forgery (SSRF) to access cloud metadata services, and finding exposed secrets in JavaScript code, source maps, debug pages and environment files.

Discovery used internet scanners, certificate transparency logs and public code sources. Those included GitHub repositories and event archives, where secrets sat outside automated revocation, plus npm and PyPI packages, container layers, notebooks, model-hosting spaces, paste sites and Telegram channels. Credentials got a grade: L1 for a lead, L2 for a working API call, L3 for measured impact such as readable data or spendable balance.

The second campaign went after identity checks at two cryptocurrency exchanges, using real-time deepfakes.

“In this campaign, threat actors used AI agents to defeat the identity verification (KYC) checks at two cryptocurrency exchanges with deepfakes.” continues the report. “The agents’ records show generating real-time deepfake video to pass live selfie checks and forged ID document photos to pass document checks.”

The agents reverse-engineered one vendor’s liveness check, which confirms a living person is present, and documented its face-detection events and coordinate system. Their best attempt put the face within 7 pixels of the target, and the vendor’s server still rejected the deepfake every time they recorded. Another session built a live deepfake pipeline on a single consumer GPU that reached 11.5 frames per second, close to what a live check expects.

They worked on ID photos too. The agents built fake camera metadata matching the real image dimensions and kept the original compression tables that re-saving would normally replace, so an edited image looked like an untouched camera photo at file level. At least four sessions ran at once, covering cash-out options, clearing balances, the deepfake pipeline and the bypass itself, and they swapped findings through shared tools. Nobody needed a coffee break.

Amazon informed the AWS customers it believed were affected by these campaigns. However, it could not confirm whether other organizations had been targeted. The company also published the IP addresses used in both campaigns, which were observed between 14 October and 2 November. Organizations can use these addresses to check their own systems for signs of activity.

Amazon advises companies to assume that attackers can quickly find exposed secrets and security weaknesses. AI applications, model gateways and inference endpoints should receive the same security protections as other production systems. Companies should also monitor unexpected costs and unusual usage.

The sessions carried commands to start and stop data tunnels, move sideways inside the operator, launch SSH, check the malware’s health and hide traces by deleting files and altering timestamps. That doesn’t match the daily scheduled script CrowdStrike described.

The malware still uses the same XOR key, but now relies on libpcap to capture network traffic. This method is less visible to standard system tools such as netstat and lsof. Another important difference is that GTP-U is also used in 5G networks, while GTP-C is not.

For interception, the attacker moves a target’s registered location to a node it controls, so later calls and texts pass through it. Enea saw two-factor codes arrive right after interception for some targets, which suggests the goal is to break into their other accounts. AI also makes it easier to create new malware.

Operators should go beyond industry checklists and monitor internal network traffic, where tools such as SIGTRANslator and Cordscan can go unnoticed. They should also connect telecom security with regular IT security. Outsourcing core network services can make investigations more difficult.

“The IPX interface must be regarded as untrusted. The evolution of signalling security has been based on the gradual realisation that the interconnect between mobile operators cannot be regarded as a trusted link.” concludes the report. “SilentAir and in particular LIMINAL PANDA exploits any available interconnect protocol, not just SS7, Diameter, GTP, but also other protocols like DNS, ICMP etc.”

The Microsoft Outage Left 15 Hotel Guests Poisoned by Chlorine

A Microsoft 365 outage stopped a hotel’s email chlorine alert in Germany, and 15 guests were poisoned.

“Over a dozen guests at a theme park hotel in Germany were injured by excessive chlorine levels.” states Notebookcheck. “According to local media reports, the incident was caused by a problem with the email-based system used to control chlorine dosing, which was affected by the Microsoft outage.”

“This is one of the issues that institutions must consider when using online services or the Internet of Things to automate systems, especially those that are crucial for safety.” states Tom’ss Hardware. “Architects of such systems should always have a physical or analog backup that will continue operating even if everything goes offline.”

A backup that nobody knows how to operate is useless. Another option is to run the servers and automation locally, rather than relying on cloud services. However, this requires more work to build and maintain, and it does not protect against local problems such as power outages.

The incident also shows how much business now depends on a few large companies. Microsoft, Google and AWS can offer reliable service at lower cost than smaller rivals or an in-house setup. When one of them goes down, millions of businesses feel it, and economic activity can stall.

For safety systems, the advice is short: architects of such systems should always have a physical or analog backup.

The reason is that the Facebook app for Android reached into photos, videos, and their metadata on Italian users’ phones without specific consent and without telling people properly what was happening.

“The Italian Data Protection Authority (Garante) has fined Meta Platforms Ireland Limited €805 million. Through the Facebook app for Android devices, the company accessed photos, videos, and related metadata stored on Italian users’ smartphones without obtaining specific consent and without properly informing them.” reads the Garante’s announcement.

“The measure concerns the ‘Get tips from your gallery while browsing Facebook’ feature, available since 2023, which, while browsing, shows users photos and videos from their phone gallery as suggestions to post.”

The feature at the center of the case suggests photos and videos from users’ phone gallery as things to post while they scroll Facebook. It has been available since 2023. The Garante opened its investigation in July 2025, after a complaint from the Bolzano Consumer Protection Centre, and backed it with its own technical analysis.

The analysis found three main problems. Facebook used a single permission to access the phone’s photo gallery for several different purposes. The notice shown to users was unclear and did not explain that granting permission would also enable photo suggestions. On top of that, the feature was enabled by default.

This is important because, once users grant full access, the app can continuously read all the photos and videos stored on their devices. Neither users nor the Garante can see exactly which data the app accesses or uses.

“The checks revealed that:

access to the gallery is requested through a single permission, valid for the entire app and for several functions serving different purposes;

the authorisation is preceded by a generic notice (“enable other features”) that does not allow users to understand that they are also authorising gallery suggestions;

furthermore, the feature was enabled by default.” continues the statement.

Meta told Reuters users must grant device-level permission to enable the feature and can revoke it at any time through their settings.

“This is a simple feature ​that helps people share photos and videos on Facebook. ​Users must grant permission on their device before the feature can operate, and ‌they ⁠can change that permission at any time in their settings,” the company spokesperson said in a statement

The Garante pointed to Article 122 of Italy’s Privacy Code, which carries the EU’s ePrivacy Directive into Italian law. That rule treats the smartphone as part of a person’s private sphere, so any access to what’s on it needs free and informed consent.

Meta argued that the Irish authority should have handled the case. The Garante said no, since the ePrivacy rules don’t include the one-stop-shop mechanism. The case also doesn’t cover whether Meta used any content to train artificial intelligence, which the company denies. The Garante did keep the option open to look separately at a new feature launched in 2026 that processes gallery content in the cloud.

Meta now has 60 days to fix things. It has to ask for consent through a separate, specific request, and explain what the feature does, that it involves gallery access, and how to turn it off. It also has to keep the feature off by default, even for people who already said yes. If Meta misses the deadline, it can’t activate the feature for anyone without valid consent.

The company also owes the Garante two reports. One is due within 30 days, on what it has started doing, and another within 90 days, on the measures it has adopted.

Several factors led to the high fine. Around 20 million Android users in Italy were affected, and the violation is still ongoing for users who have not updated the app. Meta had also been fined by the Garante in 2019 and provided only limited cooperation during the investigation.

One factor worked in Meta’s favor: the company changed the feature after the complaint. However, the Garante said these changes came too late and only applied to users who updated the app.

The fine represents about 0.47% of Meta’s global revenue. It is unlikely to cause serious financial problems for the company, but it may help give users more control over their photo privacy.

A brief history of syslog timestamps

BLUF: Debian and Ubuntu dropped a config directive in the rsyslogd package in early 2023.

As any of my FOR577 students will tell you, I'm a fan of ISO-8601 (ok, RFC 3339, too, see below). They'll probably also tell you I'm a firm believer in UTC||GTFO especially on servers (also true, and I've made stickers to prove it). I was also asked recently why the syslog timestamps in newer Ubuntu and Debian systems changed to ISO-8601 (well, probably actually RFC 3339, but I won't got down that rabbithole in this handler's diary) timestamps from the historical Mmm dd hh:mm:ss format we've known since the 1980s. I had noticed the same, a few years ago, but never really paid attention to it. Given the question from a student, I decided to do a little research on the subject and this diary is the result.

To back up some, the syslog protocol and syslogd were originally the creation of Eric Allman in 1983 because he needed a way to debug sendmail[1]. They became part of BSD 4.2 that same year. BSD 4.2 also happened to be my introduction to Unix in 1983. Syslog then made it into AT&T's System V Unix in about 1989 in SVR4 (System V Release 4) through an effort by AT&T and Sun Microsystems to try to merge the features of the 2 major branches of Unix at the time. System V adopted the protocol and routing interfaces but built the daemon on top of System V internals.

The first effort to develop the protocol into an actual standard was an IETF draft in Oct 2000 that evolved into RFC 3164[2] in 2001, by which time the protocol was actually already 18 years old. This is the syslog message format we've all come to know so well over the years. Meanwhile, around 2005, rsyslogd added the option of outputing RFC 3339 timestamps. In 2009, RFC 3164 was superceded by RFC 5424[3] which defined the timestamp to be of the format YYYY-MM-DDThh:mm:ss[.ssssss]Z or YYYY-MM-DDThh:mm:ss[.ssssss](+/-)hh:mm. Despite the new RFC, the default setting even for rsyslogd in most Linux distros continued to be to write messages to the log files in the older format (referred to as the BSD format, as specified in the title of RFC 3164), by setting a directive in /etc/rsyslog.conf (the RSYSLOG_TraditionalFileFormat directive).

That brings us to 2023, with the release of Debian 12 (Bookworm) and Ubuntu 23.04 (not a LTS release) both distros stopped installing rsyslogd by default and defaulted to the systemd journal for logging and removed the directive from the default config file for those who did install it.. Many system administrators continue to install rsyslogd because they had infrastructure in place for forwarding syslog to central log servers and/or SIEMs. These are the folks most likely to have noticed the format change. Meanwhile, Fedora and Alma (and presumably the rest of the RedHat family) have left the directive in place when the package is installed.

In syslog-ng, the new 'ISO' timestamps can be enabled by setting the following two options in syslog-ng.conf: ts-format(iso) and frac-digits(6). A couple of billion years of continued life on Earth has proved this is the only way to re-engineer complex systems while they’re usefully running. In this case, the current evolutionary endpoint that’s of most interest is the human immune system. An interlocking system of functions, it takes care of uncountably thousands of pathogen-based threats and internal malfunctions every day. You might think that the 30-odd billion computing machines making up today’s Internet are impossible to monitor and secure, but that's peanuts to the human immune system. An average human has around 30 trillion cells, and roughly the same again in bacteria. When it works, the mechanics of the human infrastructure are invisible and out of mind. The immune system makes it so, and everything involved is driven by information, signaling and behavior. There is pattern matching, resource management, defense in depth and reinforcement learning in persistent models. Sounds familiar? The analogy between this and modern digital infrastructure is such an irresistible analogy it has been around for a lifetime. Viruses, infections, swarms, worms, system health are so familiar that they go unremarked. The term Digital Immune System has been around since the last century too, pushed by outfits like IBM, Symantec and Gartner. It hasn’t taken off, probably because it’s been marketing bunkum instead of describing the development of inherently resilient infrastructure based on insights from biology. It has also been true that natural selection, the engine of evolution and this biology, only works in IT at the most macro, capitalist level of product and company survival. Everything else is artificially selected through artificial selection of designs, market analysis, and insight versus delusion. The adoption of immunological concepts to digital infrastructure will have to be the result of rigorous exploration of complex systems and cross-disciplinary research. What would that look like? There are academics thinking just these thoughts: see this paper [PDF] from Kay Smarsly at Technische Universität Hamburg. Like SCION, it takes an architectural look at what resiliency might look like, but with explicit reference to biological systems. OAAS is a chronic syndrome with multiple causes and potentially fatal outcomes. It exists and persists because the digital infrastructure itself cannot do anything about internal failures or external attacks. We have a working example of an approach to managing analogous problems in systems far vaster and more intricate than any we have built. We now have tools that may start to do systemic analysis and inference at a useful scale. In an industry prepared to bet billions on agents that do your shopping, there is every reason to kick off serious research into digital immune systems worthy of the name. Doctor, cure thyself. ®

[20] CSO Online - Clever AI prompt ideas every security leader should steal

Looking to get creative about offloading some of your risk management, compliance, and critical thinking burden to AI? These security leaders have ideas worth taking for your own.

For all the talk of AI use by attackers and defenders, little attention is paid to ways CISOs and their security pros can leverage AI uniquely to expediate tasks, cut through the noise, facilitate decision-making, and potentially save big on auditing.

Several specific AI prompts can help security leaders and staff handle multiple critical tasks not typically associated with AI for cyber defense, such as risk management, compliance, policy development, and critical thinking.

Developing truly useful prompts isn’t particularly easy, however, because it often requires a significant amount of research and effort, not to mention lots of fine-tuning, which can take security teams away from other critical tasks. Fortunately, the security community is here to help, with advice on unique prompt ideas that have greatly aided them in strengthening security, facilitating decision-making, and saving time for hands-on defense.

Create an adversarial assistant to control damage — and cut down AI slop

Dzmitry Romanovadvocates establishing an AI prompt that acts as a second security reviewer to help catch latent problems before any damage occurs. The key, though, is to give it an adversarial twist so that it looks at a situation from the attacker’s perspective while critiquing the human security reviewer’s ideas.

“What am I missing? What attack vectors or edge cases should I inspect?” is the kind of questions Romanov, cybersecurity team lead and senior security engineer at the Belarusian State University of Informatics and Radioelectronics and cybersecurity team lead with Vention, an AI-enabled software development company, such a prompt can help with.

“This approach works most effectively when analyzing architecture or code, while reviewing penetration test results, and auditing cloud configurations, as well as when modeling threats and assessing risks,” Romanov says.

Philippe Dourassov, AI pentest lead at Aikido Security, offers another version of the adversarial agent prompt — in this case against agent helpers.

When agents are used to generate security reports, engineers often find themselves buried under irrelevant findings — noise, says Dourassov. However, when a second agent is deployed to analyze the quality of another agent’s work, and correct its output, the result can be surprisingly efficient.

“The first agent has been given a task and will do everything it can to convince the humans that its results are relevant and critical, as this is what the agent would consider a good job,” Dourassov says, noting that his adversarial agent is basically a reviewer. “Its task is to eliminate noise, rectify claims, and highlight the true importance of the first agent’s result.”

The agent’s definition of a good job, Dourassov says, is being able to eliminate noise and supply only signal to the human user. “Therefore, it’s essential to use an adversarial prompt to review agents’ output, so we ensure we don’t face security fatigue from AI slop.”

Take care of business

Brandyn Fisher, senior penetration testing technical lead at business and technology consulting firm Centric Consulting, offers three prompt ideas that CISOs can deploy on a regular basis to aid with their work.

The first Fisher uses is a vendor due diligence prompt. “I feed it our organization’s risk thresholds and the vendor in question, and it benchmarks that vendor, sweeps the Internet for security pages, breach history, customer reviews, complaints, and recent news, then returns a structured report I can review and validate quickly,” he explains.

Fisher also recommends crafting a professional writing prompt that takes technical analysis and reframes it into language a CIO or board will actually engage with.

Gen AI pioneer Kevin Surace offered a similar prompt idea to attendees of this year’s CSO Conference and Awards in May. “Translate the SOC for the boardroom. This is important. They don’t really understand what’s going on,” Surace said. “The prompt here: ‘You are writing the cyber risk section of the board deck. You have four minutes of board time. Below is ninety days of security operations data. Produce three slides for a board that does not know what lateral movement means.’ This is so useful.”

Fisher’s third prompt for CISOs is a tech debt reduction prompt “where I list our technology stack and have AI flag underutilized tools, overlapping capabilities, and consolidation opportunities,” he says.

Fisher advises building each of his recommendations as a standing agent, project, or skill, or whatever your AI platform supports, so you have a static, repeatable spot to run it instead of rewriting the prompt from scratch every time.

Don’t wait for a perfect first version, Fisher adds. “Get an initial prompt working, even if it’s rough, and have AI help draft the instructions themselves.” Expect to revise it several times before it’s reliable.

Challenge your policies and content

At the CSO Conference, Surace also highlighted how CISOs can use AI prompts to audit their policy libraries.

“You are a compliance auditor. I am giving you our policy library and the current text of NIST CSF 2.0, ISO 27001:2022 Annex A, SEC Form 8-K Item 1.05, and the EU AI Act. Produce three lists,” his example prompt began, directing the LLM to find contradictions, gaps, and stale language — the kind of analysis big firms do for six figures in 12 weeks, he noted.

Olivia Brännlund, CISO at Outpost24, a cybersecurity and risk management firm, regularly uses a similar prompt that turns AI into an internal auditor and security advisor. The tool reviews policy, processes, risk assessment, incident reports, contract clauses, and specific customer requirements.

Brännlund believes that organizations that treat AI as an expert reviewer typically obtain more value than organizations that treat it as a content generator. The key, she states, “isn’t using AI to write content, but using it to challenge content.”

Tying together policy review and the adversarial assistant concept, Surace advised CSO conference attendees to also “red team your own runbook.”

“You are the operations lead for Scattered Spider. I am giving you our incident response playbook, our detection coverage matrix, and our on-call tree. Identify the five places this runbook fails,” his prompt started, probing for wrong assumptions, bottlenecks, escalation path weaknesses, and so on.

“It’s totally worth doing this,” he said. “You will have more information in an hour than you, than you’ve ever had.

Vet your agent’s capabilities and weaknesses

Chris Hughes advises asking your model what your AI agents can actually do.

“Give it an agent’s configuration, tool manifest, or MCP server definitions and ask it to summarize the agent’s effective permissions, the blast radius if it’s compromised, and whether a human approval step exists anywhere in the workflow,” says Hughes, who teaches in the CISO and Leading Enterprise Agentic AI Development Certificate Program at Carnegie Mellon University’s Heinz College. “Most organizations are deploying agents faster than they’re documenting what those agents can reach, and this closes the gap in minutes.”

The model turns work that normally requires a senior architect into something an analyst can run at design review, before deployment, and after every change. It also surfaces assumptions the team stopped questioning, Hughes says. The model doesn’t share your blind spots, so it tends to flag the over-permissioned tool or the missing approval gate that everyone walked past, he notes. Once tuned, the prompt becomes a control in its own right rather than a one-off exercise.

Embrace deep inspection

Steve Tait, CTO at cloud security firm Skyhigh Security, suggests tailoring an AI prompt to take aim at AI prompts themselves, noting that perhaps the most important capability is a prompt response inspection that understands both data and intent.

“CISOs should be able to determine not only whether sensitive information is entering or leaving an AI service, but whether a user or agent is attempting something risky—such as prompt injection, jailbreaks, policy evasion, credential exposure or unauthorized data access,” he says, adding that as AI agents become more common, that same inspection and policy model needs to extend beyond human prompts to agent-driven interactions.

Tait believes that CISOs should think of prompt security as a platform control that needs to work across every major access path to AI, including Secure Web Gateway (SWG), zero trust network access, browser-based access, and APIs.

“The goal should not be to plug a single gap for one AI application, but to establish a consistent policy and inspection layer wherever users or agents interact with AI services,” he explains. “This also means choosing an approach that can support hybrid environments, including organizations that still rely heavily on on-premises SWG infrastructure.”

## Controlling Identities for Agents

When an agent acts on an employee's behalf, controls must distinguish the agent's permissions from those of the person directing the agent to avoid inappropriate privilege escalation.

We can’t design a hospital based on the certainty that no one will ever be able to get in. We must design it so that, even when someone does get in, they can’t go anywhere. And this is probably the aspect of the transformation to Zero Trust and microsegmentation that I consider most important.

Xalgorix: Open-source AI penetration testing tool

Xalgorix, an open-source AI penetration testing tool, checks its work before it reports anything. When its main agent finds a bug, a second agent has to exploit that bug again. If the second agent can’t prove the bug is real or rule it out, Xalgorix keeps the finding and flags it for a person to review.

The check targets false positives, findings that look like vulnerabilities and turn out to be harmless. Krishna Kumar, the project’s founder, said the team measures that with internal benchmarks but has no plans yet to publish the results.

Xalgorix is a Go binary with a local web dashboard, released under the Apache-2.0 license. An LLM agent with a browser and a terminal works through a 22-phase test plan, from reconnaissance to a final report. It drives standard tools such as Nmap, Nuclei and SQLmap, and it works with seven LLM providers, including local models through Ollama. A scope guard blocks tool calls to any host outside the configured targets and their subdomains.

A second agent checks the first

“The verifier is a separate agent role, not a second pass by the agent that found the bug,” Kumar told Help Net Security. The verifier starts from a fresh context, treats the hunting agent’s proof as untrusted and gets read-only tools. Each check is capped at eight turns and three minutes.

The verifier sets a different bar for each bug class. “XSS: the script has to execute. Reflection alone doesn’t count,” Kumar said. Server-side request forgery (SSRF), which tricks a server into making requests for an attacker, needs “an HTTP callback from the target’s server to a fresh token, with redirects disabled. A DNS-only hit, or a callback that came from the scanner itself, doesn’t count.”

The code also overrules the model. “If the model returns ‘confirmed’ without a successful re-test of its own, the code downgrades the verdict to inconclusive,” Kumar said. The verifier drops a finding only on positive disproof, such as by-design behavior. A bug that needs a second account, or triggers out of the verifier’s view, lands in the manual review pile.

The README compares Xalgorix with Nuclei, OWASP ZAP and commercial DAST tools, but only in broad terms. It describes how each one works and doesn’t include any test results. Kumar described two internal tests. One pairs small vulnerable apps with secure builds of the same feature, so any bug reported on a secure build counts as a false positive. The other runs real CVEs in containers next to their patched releases, such as Grafana 8.2.6 and 8.2.7 for CVE-2021-43798. A run passes only if every repeat finds the CVE and leaves the patched version clean.

The team has also started on the XBOW validation benchmarks. “We haven’t run all 104 yet, so I don’t have a full score to share,” Kumar said. “We haven’t planned to publish benchmark results yet.”

Models, and a root container

Claude Opus 5.5 has given the best results, Kumar said. “A full run is a long chain of tool calls, and weaker models start producing malformed tool calls or lose track of state as the context grows,” he said. Xalgorix recovers from malformed output, he added, but local models still need more supervision on long scans.

The Docker image runs as root and needs --privileged or a list of added capabilities. The maintainers tell users to treat the container as a disposable sandbox on an isolated network.

Google’s semantic governance policies can block an AI agent’s tool call before it runs

Google lets enterprises put a checkpoint in front of an AI agent‘s actions. Semantic governance policies, in Preview on the Gemini platform, look at each tool call the agent’s model proposes and block it before it runs if the call doesn’t match the user’s request or breaks a company rule. The check sits outside the agent, at an enforcement point such as Agent Gateway.

Permissions list the actions an agent is able to take and leave open whether a particular call follows your rules. A model can be talked out of the rules in its system instructions by a user with a good reason, or by a document that tells it to do something the user did not request, a technique called indirect prompt injection.

A language model acts as the judge. It compares each call the agent’s model proposes against two things: rules your legal or compliance team writes in plain language, and what the user asked for. The user-intent check is on by default and needs no policies. A call has to pass both. If it passes, the response reaches the agent unchanged. If it fails, the engine swaps the response, tool call included, for a denial message and the call does not run. Verdicts and the judge’s rationale go to Cloud Logging. Streamed model responses are not evaluated.

*   _18/11/2026 --- v1.0 -- Initial publication_

## Summary

On 18 November 2026, F5 published an advisory addressing five vulnerabilities in NGINX that allow an unauthenticated remote attacker to execute code with the privileges of the NGINX worker process [1, 2].

While researchers managed to successfully demonstrate the use of CVE-2026-123007 [1], no exploitation in the wild has been reported yet.

It is recommended to swiftly update NGINX as soon as possible, prioritising internet facing appliances.

## Technical Details

The vulnerability **CVE-2026-123007**, with a CVSS score of 8.1, is an integer overflow in HTTP/2 flow control which causes an out-of-bounds write on the heap. The only precondition is HTTP/2 enabled on a listening port.

The vulnerability **CVE-2026-123633**, with a CVSS score of 8.1, is a use-after-free flaw occurring when connection draining closes an active HTTP/2 connection while NGINX connects to an upstream server. HTTP/2 should be enabled and requests should be forwarded to an upstream server.

The vulnerability **CVE-2026-121810**, with a CVSS score of 8.1, is a use-after-free flaw occurring when a QUIC connection is drained during stream creation and upstream connection. HTTP/3 should be enabled and requests should be forwarded to an upstream server.

The vulnerability **CVE-2026-123634**, with a CVSS score of 8.1, is a use-after-free flaw occurring in the processing of the DNS resolver waiting list. a `resolver` must be set, a `proxy_pass` destination built from request data, and `mirror` with `mirror_request_body off` should be configured.

The vulnerability **CVE-2026-113425**, with a CVSS score of 8.1, is a use-after-free occurring when the client closes the connection during the `auth_http` authentication lookup. A mail proxy must be configured with `proxy on` and `auth_http`.

All five vulnerabilities can lead to unauthenticated code execution with the privileges of the NGINX worker process [1].

## Affected Products

The following products and versions are affected by one or more of the above vulnerabilities:

*   NGINX Open Source (mainline) from version 1.9.5 to 1.31.8 (range depends on the vulnerability, see Technical Details) [1]. Refer to the vendor advisory for the information on fixed versions [2].
*   NGINX Open Source (stable) versions 1.30.x, including the current stable version 1.30.6 [1, 3]. Refer to the vendor advisory for the information on fixed versions [2].
*   NGINX Plus from version 37.0.0 to 37.0.7.1, and 37.1.0 to 37.2.0.1. The versions R34, R35 and R36 are also affected [2]. Refer to the vendor advisory for the information on fixed versions [2].

## Recommendations

CERT-EU recommends updating vulnerable instance as soon as possible, prioritising internet-facing devices.

### Compromise assessment

It is possible to monitor for crashes of the Nginx worker (`worker process … exited on signal` in the error log). However, this is not a high-fidelity indicator of successful remote code execution but rather an indication of exploitation attempts. Further checks in the process creation logs are necessary.

### Mitigation

When updating is not immediately possible, disabling HTTP/2 on exposed listeners (by removing the `http2` from the `listen` directives, or set `http2 off;`) mitigates the vulnerabilities CVE-2026-123007 and CVE-2026-123633 [1].

Der Cybersicherheitsmonitor zeigt: Von Cyberkriminalität Betroffene erfahren am häufigsten Betrug beim Onlineshop, im Jahr 2026 waren das allein 22 Prozent. Cyberkriminelle nutzen dabei auch Rabattaktionen gezielt aus, um an Geld und persönliche Daten zu gelangen. Dafür verwenden sie z.B. sogenannte Fakeshops: Diese sehen auf den ersten Blick wie seriöse Onlineshops aus. Wer in einem Fakeshop bestellt, erhält trotz Bezahlung jedoch möglicherweise keine Ware. Überprüfen Sie den Onlineshop! Auf der Webseite sollten Sie Telefonnummer und E-Mail-Adresse sowie ein vollständiges Impressum finden. Indizien für einen Fakeshop sind etwa kostenpflichtige Telefonnummern oder die Beschränkung auf ein Kontaktformular.

Achten Sie auf bekannte Gütesiegel! Beim Klick auf das Siegel sollten Sie zu weiteren Informationen gelangen. Fakeshops erfinden mitunter eigene Siegel: Diese sind dann jedoch oftmals nicht klickbar.

Hinterfragen Sie Angebote kritisch! Extreme niedrige Preise und außergewöhnlich hohe Rabatte können Warnsignale sein. Ein Preisvergleich bei mehreren Anbietern hilft bei der Einschätzung.

Kaufen Sie nicht unter Zeitdruck ein! Lassen Sie sich auch von Countdown-Anzeigen und angeblich knappen Lagerbeständen nicht zu unüberlegten Käufen verleiten.

Wählen Sie sichere Zahlungsmethoden! Bei unbekannten Shops ist Vorsicht geboten, wenn ausschließlich Vorkasse per Überweisung angeboten wird. Besser sind Zahlungsarten mit geeigneten Schutzmöglichkeiten, beispielsweise der Kauf auf Rechnung.

Wer den Verdacht hat, in einem Fakeshop eingekauft zu haben, sollte unverzüglich handeln: Das BSI empfiehlt, den Zahlungsdienstleister beziehungsweise die Bank zu kontaktieren und die Möglichkeiten einer Rückbuchung zu prüfen. Zudem sollten Belege und Bestellbestätigungen gesichert und Anzeige erstattet werden. Wurden Zugangsdaten preisgegeben, sollten betroffene Passwörter zudem umgehend geändert werden.

Auf der Webseite des BSI finden Verbraucherinnen und Verbraucher weitere Informationen und praktische Sicherheitstipps rund um Onlineshopping.

No Sleep, No Breaks, No Human Help. Amazon Watched AI Agents Hack a Server

Autonomous AI agents were given one instruction: “obtain shell access.” Nobody told them which vulnerability to use or what to try next. Over about two days, they probed university servers, built their own lists of usernames, ran for hours without a break and, on one target, found a previously unknown flaw and used it to take full control of a server.

Amazon Threat Intelligence says this is what an AI-run hacking campaign already looks like, and it believes a Chinese-nexus threat actor was behind it.

CJ Moses, CISO of Amazon Integrated Security, shared the findings on the AWS blog.

How Amazon Found the Autonomous AI Agents

On September 10, 2026, Amazon found a server running a public instance of Cairn, an open-source AI penetration-testing framework, with no authentication. Because the operator left it open, anyone on the internet could see its full history, including project settings, the actor’s prompts and the AI’s output.

The prompts were written in Simplified Chinese, and timestamps in the scripts were set to China Standard Time. The records showed a campaign against IP addresses linked to universities in East Asia outside mainland China, over about 48 hours starting September 8.

Cairn was built as a research and problem-solving tool for defenders. A user gives it a starting point and a goal in plain language, and it coordinates multiple AI agents to find a way to reach that goal.

Amazon says that same simplicity makes it attractive to attackers. In this campaign, the actor supplied a list of target IP addresses and variations of the “obtain shell access” goal. The agents then planned, acted, and adjusted as they learned, without the operator choosing each step.

The Attacks That Failed

Not every operation worked, but the failures show how persistent the agents were.

Against a university Redis database that allowed access without a password, the agents spent about 16 hours on 148 actions in a row. They worked through a long list of known techniques, including trying to load unauthorised code, escape a scripting sandbox, and corrupt memory. When those failed, they mapped the internal network through a flaw in another service and tried logging in to several systems. Nothing worked.

In a separate six-hour operation against a university GPU server, the agents built a list of about 80 real usernames from faculty directories, lab websites, archived pages and academic papers. They then tried more than 30,000 password combinations across the server and other university systems, including a VPN and the campus portal. None succeeded.

Amazon notes that, together, these attempts show the agents kept adapting for almost a full day with no human direction.

How the AI Agents Found a Zero-Day

The result was different against a server running an open-source medical imaging AI application.

The agents identified the app’s framework, mapped its API, and found a file upload feature that accepted a filename. They tested it for path traversal, a flaw that lets an attacker write files outside the intended folder, and it worked. In about 11 minutes, they went from mapping the API to writing files anywhere on the server.

In a later session, they used the same flaw to plant a hidden Python payload in a file that Python loads automatically at startup. They then triggered a feature that launched a new Python process, which ran the payload and opened a reverse shell with root access, giving them full control.

The flaw had no security advisory and no patch. Amazon assesses with high confidence that it was a zero-day, since it found no fix, issue, advisory, or earlier mention of it anywhere.

Where the Human Stepped In

The actor took over at one point. After the agents found the zero-day, the actor gave them a proxy tunnelling into a second university network. The server on the other end ran the same vulnerable software, and Amazon infers the actor had used the zero-day to break into it. The agents were then told to target other machines on that network.

Amazon assesses with moderate confidence that the actor is Chinese-nexus. It bases this on the Simplified Chinese prompts, the China Standard Time timestamps and the choice of universities likely of strategic interest to China.

It is also not the only group using Cairn. Gambit Security has separately documented a financially motivated actor using it against online retailers, which suggests the tool is being picked up for both espionage and crime.

Amazon told the open-source project’s maintainers about the flaw, and they released a fix and a GitHub Security Advisory. The affected universities were not AWS customers, so AWS worked with a government partner to notify them. Amazon also published two IP addresses seen in the activity between September 8 and 10.

Why It Matters for Defenders

Amazon’s main warning is about speed. An agent that finds and exploits a zero-day in minutes can finish before many security teams have even looked at the alert. The agents that failed against the Redis server ran for 16 hours straight, with no breaks or working hours.

To keep up, Amazon recommends that organisations rely on high-confidence detections that trigger automated responses, with people overseeing the outcomes rather than reviewing every individual action.
